UNVEIL
Case study

A habit game where keeping your word to yourself unveils a picture, one piece at a time. How it was designed and built — and what I decided not to build.

Solo project Flutter · Dart · SQLite Jul – Sep 2026 Android · Windows · iOS in progress

01The question

Opening the app has to be worth more than not opening it

“Opening the app has to carry more incentive than not opening it.” — a mentoring conversation, August 2026

Most habit apps answer this with pressure — streak flames, guilt notifications, a widget that displays your failure. I did not want to build that. The apps I have deleted, I deleted because I could feel them managing me.

So I looked for a different answer, and what I found was not a feature but a definition:

Unveil gives feedback to behavior that has none.

Push-ups show nothing for weeks. Twenty pages of reading leave no trace. The reward for keeping a small promise is invisible, so the promise loses to whatever is louder. Unveil makes it visible: a picture you chose comes apart, a little, because you did the thing.

That sentence decided everything after it: if the app has to manufacture a feeling to keep you, it has already failed.

02The decision everything rests on

Do not slice the image

The obvious build is to cut the picture into N tiles and reveal them one by one. I cut nothing. The image stays whole and untouched; the app draws a cover over it. Progress is exactly two values.

Freezing the permutation matters as much as not slicing. Because it is written once, the same goal unveils in the same order forever — on replay, after a restore from backup, on another device.

Everything after it got cheap. Cinematic reveal (hundreds of pieces on a percentage schedule), the end-of-journey replay, the head start that opens a few pieces the moment you commit, the partial-win archive that freezes a picture at 84% — each one is a function answering “how many pieces today?” No image pipeline, no re-encoding, no per-goal derived assets.

03Failure is delay, not punishment

The app never takes back what you earned

A missed day reveals nothing and pushes the finish date back by one. It never takes pieces away.

This is the rule the whole product hangs on. An app that can take back what you earned has to be obeyed. An app that only ever waits can be returned to. Three contract modes sit on top of it — Standard (a miss costs a day), Relaxed (no failure at all), Deadline (a fixed end date; finish 80% or more and it is framed as an honest partial win rather than a fake trophy).

The verdicts are deliberately unflattering — PERFECT / PARTIAL / FAILED / SKIPPED. The tone should be gentle; the record should not be. Those are two different problems.

04The promise is a test, not a sentence

When a public promise had to be reversed

Generation 1 shipped with “no tracking” on the store page and no internet permission at all. Generation 2 needed data. Reversing a promise that is already public is the part most projects handle by editing a paragraph in a policy document.

I handled it three ways.

  1. Three modes, identical features. Personal sends nothing; Core and Full send more. None of them unlocks a feature the others lack — consent traded for functionality is not freely given under GDPR Art. 7(4), and gating on it is precisely the coercion this product exists to reject.
  2. Shape leaves; content never does. The split below is the whole contract.
  3. The list is executable. plan(mode, allowed) is the single source of truth for what a payload may contain, and data_consent_test fails the build if a forbidden field ever enters one. The promise does not depend on my memory of having made it.

May leave — shape

  • whether the day succeeded
  • pledge count, contract mode, duration
  • days since the last one
  • whether a note was used, and how often edited
  • whether the letter was opened or skipped

Never leaves, in any mode — content

  • the goal title, the pledge text
  • the journal, the letter, the free note
  • the picture, its filename, any image data
  • exact dates, device identifiers, IP
Nothing about IP is stored. Until 2026-09-18 a seven-day rotating-salt hash was kept for abuse detection with a promise that it was never joined to analytics — and the only thing enforcing that promise was a comment, while the value was never once used. So it was removed outright; if abuse ever needs an answer, Cloud Run's own request logs hold the IP for their retention window. The install identifier was built before the first transmission — you cannot promise deletion of something that does not exist yet.

05What I chose not to build

The rejected list says more about a product than the shipped one

Rejected Why
Subscription Insights are the retention engine. Locking them means the app has to keep you in order to bill you, and from then on every design question gets answered wrong. All free.
Server-side gifting The elegant version is a hash and a server. That turns the app into a UGC platform — moderation duty, abuse vectors, and a tracking surface I had just promised not to build. Gifts ship as a file sent through the user's own messenger: the trust sits between two people, not routed through me.
Weekly / monthly judgment Kinder — and a different product. Daily is the identity.
Firebase / GA4 Would have contradicted the store page outright. Visibility came from opt-in stats and a feedback form instead.
Selling higher limits Five goals and ten pledges are not a paywall, they are the message.
A guardian companion I built it — a silent state function, four moods, appears when the mark is tapped. Then I removed it. A timecapsule letter you write to yourself does the same job better: when you drift, the voice that meets you is your own, not a character the app invented. It was also the one thing in the app with a personality, which made it the least Unveil-like thing in it.

06Where I was wrong

  • I recommended not recording counter usage — having silently merged two decisions, “don't record” and “don't touch the schema.” Separated, the answer flips: unrecorded data is gone forever; a migration is a Tuesday.
  • I pushed for the picture to carry heavier meaning. Heavy meaning turns a missed day into a betrayal, which fights the forgiveness the entire design stands on.
  • I over-weighted server region as a compliance lever. Genuinely anonymous data is outside those rules to begin with.
  • I looked for research on when a lapsed user is most likely to return and found nothing worth citing. The three-day trigger is a judgment call, and it is documented as one rather than dressed up as a finding.

08Engineering notes

  • Flutter / Dart, Riverpod, drift (SQLite), a self-built localization layer (Korean / English). Animation is CustomPaint plus Tickers.
  • Fifteen schema migrations, none destructive. The same install has been dogfooded continuously since schema v2 — a migration that cannot survive a real device is not a migration.
  • Domain logic (verdicts, reveal amounts, rhythm, the consent contract) is pure and is the layer under test. 84 tests hold the rules in code rather than in policy.
  • Every change: analyze → test → release build → actually run it. A passing build is not a claim that it works.
  • Two bugs worth remembering: a settings load race that replayed an unlock celebration on every launch, and a double-registration race between two catch-up paths. Both were fixed by removing concurrency, not by adding guards.
  • Local-first by default — no account, and generation 1 shipped without the internet permission. Backup is a zip the user carries out, and the install identifier is deliberately stored outside it.

09Status

Generation 1 (v1.1.0) is complete and archived — core loop, gallery, insights, activity calendar, gifting, Korean/English, Android and Windows. Generation 2 is in development; Google Play closed testing is next.
